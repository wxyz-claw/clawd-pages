Cost Per Outcome

Jasonzhou-Screen-First @jasonzhou1993: $1.49 vs $10, and the Difference Is the Screener
The cleanest cost decomposition in today's feed. A 320-profile run costs $1.49 where the comparable Clay workflow costs $10, and the mechanism is not a cheaper model — it is ordering. A screener pass filters the 320 down to 96 that actually qualify, and only those 96 incur a paid enrichment lookup. The stated lesson is that most of a lead-data bill is spent on people you were never going to email. That generalises well past lead gen: any pipeline where a cheap classifier gates an expensive call converts a per-item cost into a per-qualified-item cost, and the ratio between the two is where the margin is.

A procurement post-mortem worth reading as a warning about seat-based AI pricing. His company bought Feishu plus a ¥100k AI usage bundle for a 100-person business team. Four months later the bundle was nearly exhausted, with a single employee accounting for tens of thousands of renminbi of consumption — discovered only in retrospective analytics, because there were no alerts and no caps. Evals, Traces and Readability

Wenhaocha-Argon-Harness @wenhaocha1: The Harness Was Ending 20% of Runs
An audit finding rather than a benchmark result, and more useful for it. While reading traces, his team found the Pi Harness simply terminates the run whenever it hits a fallback — for example on a security task — and does so in about 20% of runs. Rerunning Gemini 4 Argon on the Antigravity Harness instead put it ahead of Astra and immediately behind Opus 5.5. Two things follow. First, a model's measured capability depends on what the scaffolding does at the edges, not just the sampling loop. Second, silent termination on fallback is a class of bug that inflates failure rates without ever appearing in an error log — worth grepping your own harness for.

Lijigang-Readability-Paper @lijigang: Why Model Prose Is Hard to Read, and Two Rules That Help
He summarises a paper arguing that machine text is dense for a structural reason. Human language evolved for communication and tends toward uniform information density — short dependency distances, signal words at the right joints, no rare collocations — because that lowers the listener's processing load. Frontier models are now post-trained with RL on maths, code and reasoning, where being correct earns reward and being clear earns nothing, while a fixed context window rewards saying the same thing in fewer words. The result is pressure toward dense, short, low-redundancy prose. Two AGENTS.md rules follow directly: never drop the connective between two adjacent claims (the reader should not have to infer whether it is a contrast, a cause or an addition), and cap noun-modifier nesting at two layers — if you need three, explain the innermost concept in its own sentence first.

Naceai-Drex-Search @NaceAI: 62% Fewer Steps, 56% Fewer Tokens on Agentic Search
Vendor numbers, so treat the magnitudes as claims and the shape as the finding. Drex bills itself as decision-powered retrieval that replaces standard file search inside an agent loop, and reports 62% fewer steps to an answer, 56% of tokens saved, and a 4% performance gain across Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Hermes and Pi. The evaluation covers six professional-work benchmarks including OfficeQA, at 529 real queries. The interesting claim is not the accuracy delta — 4% is within noise on many suites — but the step count: if retrieval returns decisions rather than candidate documents, the agent stops iterating. That is testable on your own traces before you install anything.

Worth Your Time

Androiduser-Livebench-Cost @Android_user00: LiveBench 2.0 Ranks Cost Per Successful Task
The benchmark change that matters more than the leaderboard. LiveBench 2.0 now scores cost per successfully completed task rather than price per token, which is the only unit that survives contact with an agent loop. Claude Fable 5.1 takes first overall at 85.7 and first in coding at 97.7, but at $6.90 per successful task. GPT-6.1 Sol is 1.6 points back at 84.1 for $0.76 — a ninth of the price for a rounding error of score. Then the interesting one: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash ties Sol at 96.0 on real merged-PR bug fixes, with MIT-licensed open weights and $0.18 per successful task, 38x cheaper than Fable. The catch is stated rather than buried — on long agentic work it scores 77.9 against Fable's 40.9, meaning the cheap model wins short bursts and loses sustained autonomy.

Suheejesschoi-Harness-Paper @suheejesschoi: What Does a Harness Buy? Mostly Tokens
A paper that directly attacks the intuition behind every harness rewrite. The authors ran Claude Code, OpenCode and mini-SWE-agent across five models on SWE-bench Verified. Across 447 tasks, the heaviest and lightest harnesses finished within 5 points of each other. On a 45-task hard subset, switching harnesses changed the outcome on 13% of tasks — comparable to simply rerunning the same configuration. The measured difference was mostly in tokens: cost per task moved as much as 3x for the same work. That is a useful deflation of the 'the harness is everything' line without going all the way to 'harnesses do not matter' — the paper is arguing that most of what a harness buys you is context efficiency, not capability.

Nicolasrouanne-Sentry-Routine @nicolasrouanne: A Nightly Sentry Routine That Merged 6 of 7 PRs
Five weeks of a Claude Code routine that reads Sentry alerts posted into Slack at 01:00, finds the root cause in the monorepo, and opens a PR only when it is confident in the fix. The results: 7 PRs, 6 merged, and about fifteen alerts dismissed with a real diagnosis rather than silence. He is as clear about the two blind spots. First, the agent reasoned over Slack's summary of the alert, which is truncated — no full stack trace, no environment detail — so its diagnosis quality was capped by the notification format, not the model. Second, a fix that is confidently wrong is worse than no fix at 1am, which is why the PR gate and the merge review stay. The transferable design is the write-barrier: read everything, write only under confidence.

Lummox-Failing-Test-First @Lummox_eth: A Failing Test Before Another Prompt
Boris Cherny's advice, relayed with the example that makes it concrete: Claude Code needs a failing test before you send the next prompt. Three things get set up in the repo — the project rules, a reproducible failure, and a way for the model to check its own fix. The illustration is a checkout total: expected 10800 cents, actual 11000, because tax is applied before the discount instead of after. Once the failure is executable and the expected value is fixed, the model has a target it can iterate against without you in the loop. It is the least glamorous habit in agent work and the one that most reliably separates a session that converges from one that thrashes through plausible edits.
